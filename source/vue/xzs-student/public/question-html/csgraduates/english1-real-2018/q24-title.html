Among the annoying challenges facing the middle class is one that will probably go unmentioned in the next presidential campaign: What happens when the robots come for their jobs?

Don’t dismiss that possibility entirely. About half of U.S. jobs are at high risk of being automated, according to a University of Oxford study, with the middle class disproportionately squeezed. Lower-income jobs like gardening or day care don’t appeal to robots. But many middle-class occupations-trucking, financial advice, software engineering–have aroused their interest, or soon will. The rich own the robots, so they will be fine.

This isn’t to be alarmist. Optimists point out that technological upheaval has benefited workers in the past. The Industrial Revolution didn’t go so well for Luddites whose jobs were displaced by mechanized looms, but it eventually raised living standards and created more jobs than it destroyed. Likewise, automation should eventually boost productivity, stimulate demand by driving down prices, and free workers from hard, boring work. But in the medium term, middle-class workers may need a lot of help adjusting.

The first step, as Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee argue in The Second Machine Age, should be rethinking education and job training. Curriculums from grammar school to college should evolve to focus less on memorizing facts and more on creativity and complex communication. Vocational schools should do a better job of fostering problem-solving skills and helping students work alongside robots. Online education can supplement the traditional kind. It could make extra training and instruction affordable. Professionals trying to acquire new skills will be able to do so without going into debt.

The challenge of coping with automation underlines the need for the U.S. to revive its fading business dynamism: Starting new companies must be made easier. In previous eras of drastic technological change, entrepreneurs smoothed the transition by dreaming up ways to combine labor and machines. The best uses of 3D printers and virtual reality haven’t been invented yet. The U.S. needs the new companies that will invent them.

Finally, because automation threatens to widen the gap between capital income and labor income, taxes and the safety net will have to be rethought. Taxes on low-wage labor need to be cut, and wage subsidies such as the earned income tax credit should be expanded: This would boost incomes, encourage work, reward companies for job creation, and reduce inequality.

Technology will improve society in ways big and small over the next few years, yet this will be little comfort to those who find their lives and careers upended by automation. Destroying the machines that are coming for our jobs would be nuts. But policies to help workers adapt will be indispensable.

在困扰中产阶级的烦人挑战中，有一个挑战很可能不会在下一届总统竞选活动中被提及，那就是：如果机器人抢了他们的工作，会发生什么情况？不要完全忽视这种可能性。牛津大学的一项研究显示，美国约一半的工作面临被自动化的高风险，中产阶级受到不成比例的挤压。园艺或日托等低收入工作对机器人没有吸引力。但许多中产阶级的职业，如卡车驾驶、金融咨询、软件工程等，已经引起或很快将引起机器人的兴趣。富人拥有机器人，所以他们不会有问题。

这并不是危言耸听。乐观主义者指出，过去的技术变革使工人受益。工业革命对于勒德分子来说并不顺利，因为他们的工作被机械织布机取代，但工业革命最终提高了生活水平，并且创造的就业机会比破坏的更多。同样，自动化最终将提高生产力，通过压低价格刺激需求，把工人从艰苦、枯燥的工作中解放出来。但中期而言，中产阶级工人可能需要很多帮助才能适应。

正如埃里克•布赖恩约弗森和安德鲁•麦卡菲在《第二个机器时代》一书中所述，第一步应该是反思教育和职业培训。从文法学校到大学的课程应该有所发展，减少对记忆事实的关注，更加关注创造力和复杂的沟通。职业学校应该更好地培养解决问题的能力，帮助学生与机器人一起工作。在线教育可以补充传统教育。在线教育将使人们负担得起额外的培训和指导。想要学习新技能的专业人士将能够在不负债的情况下参加培训。

应对自动化的挑战凸显了美国重振其日益消退的商业活力的必要性：必须使创办新公司变得更为容易。在以往技术发生巨变的时代，企业家设想出把劳动力和机器结合起来的方法，从而平稳完成过渡。目前尚未发明出充分利用3D打印机和虚拟现实的最佳方法。美国需要将发明这些方法的新公司。

最后，由于自动化有可能扩大资本收入与劳动收入之间的差距，因此必须反思税收和安全网规则。需要削减对低收入劳动的征税，并且应该扩大劳动所得税减免等工资补贴。这将增加收入，鼓励工作，奖励创造就业的公司，并减少不平等。

未来几年，技术将以或大或小的方式改善社会，然而这对那些生活和职业生涯被自动化颠覆的人起不到什么安慰作用。摧毁来抢我们工作的机器将是非常愚蠢的。但帮助工人适应的政策将是不可或缺的。
